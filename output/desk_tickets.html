Before I evaluate anything, I want to start with the miss that bothers me most, because it isn't a cash error and the board wouldn't catch it. Our Customer Service agent leaked internal business details into both customer drafts. The Ticket 101 draft tells Tauhid Zaman that his tee is "awaiting payment approval" and that "the payment request for invoice 501 is awaiting human 2FA approval". Every number in those drafts is true, and every number came from an MCP tool, so the "never invent data" rule held. But a true fact can still be the wrong fact to share with a customer. The only thing that kept this from becoming a real incident is the rule I set at the start: drafts stay on board (draft_message writes to output/drafts/ and never sends). That rule did exactly the job it was designed to do.

1. How I would evaluate the agents' performance on each ticket, and why

I chose a pass/fail checklist over a single score. At Campus Customs, some failures are disqualifying no matter how good the rest is. A wrong balance in cash_accounts, a payment without human 2FA, or a message actually sent to a customer can't be averaged away by good routing. Each check below maps to a rule in RULES.md or a line in the Expected plan, and it can be verified from the working database, output/audit_trail.json, or the drafts folder. It doesn't depend on the agents' own description of what they did. The checks fall into four groups:

  • Money (the most important): was the cash change correct and approved by a human?
  • Safety: did anything leave the building, and did the balance stay positive?
  • Process: was the tier right, were only the needed staff involved, and was the work done without wasted calls?
  • Outcome quality: would I send the draft as written, and does the ticket status reflect reality?

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why

My overall view is that the team was worth it, with caveats. The run data on this page shows where the team earned its complexity and where a single agent with the same 16 MCP tools would have been simpler.

What would have been simpler as one agent:

  • Ticket 102 end to end. A routine rent notice is three tool calls: check_rent_due(1) → preview_payment("rent",1) → pay_rent(1). The team needed 2 agents, a hand-off, 10 model requests and 9 tool calls to get there. A single agent would have had the lease facts in its own context instead of re-sending them to Accounting in a delegation message. It would also have been less likely to call pay_rent twice, since that duplicate came from Accounting re-asking after the pause.
  • Fact-gathering on 101 and 103. On 103, the IT Director asked Inventory for stock, then Customer Service asked Inventory again (16 tool calls, $0.0076, the most expensive run). Every hand-off is a lossy summary: the IT Director's first message even said the SKU wasn't in its brief. One agent calling check_stock_and_price, check_vendor_can_ship and quote_bulk_discount in sequence would have had all the numbers in one place, and probably would have caught that 62% headroom existed.
  • Consistency of voice. The 101 draft repeated Accounting's internal language ("awaiting human 2FA approval") because the IT Director passed P-0003's status to Customer Service in the hand-off, and Customer Service treated it as something to tell the customer. A single agent knows which facts are internal and which are customer-facing, and writes one coherent story.

Why I still think the team was worth it:

  • Least privilege on money. Only Accounting holds pay_invoice and pay_rent. Inventory, Facilities and Customer Service can't move cash, even by mistake. With one agent, every tool sits in one context, and the only thing between a misread ticket and a payment is the approval gate.
  • Routing kept the work small. The tiers sent 102 to one staff member with no director, and 103 never touched Accounting. The Expected plan predicted that, and the Actual tabs show it happened.
  • Auditability. Because each step is tagged with an agent and depth in audit_trail.json, I can see that the leaked cost figures came from Customer Service on a hand-off from the IT Director, and that's where to fix the prompt. One agent's log would be one long transcript.
  • Scale. With three tickets this is overbuilt. With thirty open tickets on a Monday of move-in week, triage → Tier 1 staff is how I'd want work divided.

The caveat is that the money safety didn't actually come from having multiple agents. It came from the tools: the signed approval, the $50 2FA threshold, the cash refusal and draft-only messaging live in the MCP server, so one agent would have been just as safe on money. The team's real value is in least privilege, routing and the audit trail, and those only pay off at higher volume.

4. Three new problems this agent team could solve with the tools we built

  1. A student orders 3 Yale Caps. triage_ticket would match KI-ORDER-INSTOCK (40 CC-HAT-BLUE on hand), so it's Tier 1, straight to Customer Service.
    • Customer Service runs check_stock_and_price("CC-HAT-BLUE","OS",3) ($22 list, $6 cost), drafts a confirmation with draft_message, and closes the ticket with update_ticket.
    • One agent, no director, no money. It's the cheapest path in the playbook, and the opposite of ticket 101's four-agent chain.
  2. A customer orders a Crest Mug, which is out of stock (0 on hand). Unlike the tee in 101, the restock vendor isn't blocked, so this is KI-ORDER-RESTOCK, Tier 1: Inventory → Customer Service.
    • Inventory uses list_vendors to pick Elm City Gifts ("mugs and small goods", 3 lead days, no open invoices) and check_vendor_can_ship(2), which gives an earliest arrival of 2026-09-03.
    • Customer Service drafts that date to the customer.
    • It shows the team handling a "101-style" order without the Accounting detour, because the blocker isn't there.
  3. QuickShip CT bills a $35 courier invoice, for example to deliver Tauhid's tee once it arrives, entered into invoices. Accounting can preview_payment and pay_invoice.
    • Because $35 is under the $50 two-factor threshold, a single human approval is enough, while P-0003 and P-0004 needed 2FA.
    • preview_payment would show $160.00 → $125.00, so it still fits the cash we have left on the Cash tab.
    • It's a good test that the threshold logic behaves differently below $50.

5. Three new problems this agent team could not resolve with our tools, and what we'd need

  1. The October rent: $2,400 due 2026-10-02 with $160 in checking. The Cash tab already shows this coming. The team can see the problem: triage_ticket would push it to Tier 3 (Boss) as a cash conflict, and preview_payment would return enough_cash: false. But it can't fix it.
    • The pay tool will (correctly) refuse, and there's no revenue in our model: no sales table, and no way to record that the 8 hoodies or 40 caps were sold.
    • Tools needed: a sales/receipts table with record_sale (so cash can go up), a forecast_cash(days) tool to warn before approving P-0004 that it would leave $160 against the next rent, and possibly a request_payment_plan draft to Elm City Properties, approved by a human.
  2. Actually ordering the 12 missing hoodies for the Yale AI Club. Ticket 103 stopped at "short 12", because there's nothing to place an order with. The approve route only covers invoices and rent, and "purchase" doesn't exist in the database.
    • Tools needed:
      • a purchase_orders table
      • create_purchase_order(vendor_id, sku, size, qty), priced from vendor quotes rather than assuming pricing.unit_cost (12 × $22 = $264, which is more than the $160 we have)
      • a human-approval and 2FA step for purchases like the one for payments
      • receive_shipment to add to inventory.qty when Bulldog delivers
    • Then 103 could become a real order instead of a draft.
  3. Closing the loop with customers: sending the reviewed draft, getting the Yale AI Club's "yes", and honoring an approved price. Today draft_message only writes a file, there's no inbox, and there's no table for an approved price override on an order.
    • Tools needed:
      • send_message, gated by human approval (and 2FA, per our safety rule on contacting customers)
      • an inbox tool that turns replies into tickets
      • approve_price_override(ticket_id, unit_price) plus an orders table
    • A draft-review guardrail: just as important, given my biggest miss, a review_draft tool that checks a draft against a block-list (unit cost, margin, invoice numbers, "overdue", "2FA") before a human ever sees it.
    • Writing "don't mention internal cash" in a prompt wasn't enough. It needs to be enforced in code, the way the cash rules are.

What I'd change first

  1. Move the soft rules into tools. Add a draft redaction check in draft_message (no cost, margin, invoice or approval language to customers), and refuse a price_override reply that ignores the requested discount without giving a reason.
  2. Separate "desk done" from "customer done". Add a status like waiting_on_vendor or offer_pending that the board treats as finished for the agents but not resolved for the customer. Then 101 and 103 wouldn't look finished when Tauhid has no shirt and the club has no offer.
  3. Look ahead before paying. Have preview_payment report cash after all upcoming obligations, so my approval of P-0004 would have shown "this leaves $160 against $2,400 due 2026-10-02" right on the approval card.
